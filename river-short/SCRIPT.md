On June twenty-second, nineteen sixty-nine, the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland caught fire. Literally.
It had burned a dozen times before, worst in nineteen fifty-two, over a million dollars in damage.
Decades of oil had turned its surface into a greasy slick.

That Sunday, a freight train crossed the Campbell Road bridge.
Sparks fell from its wheels onto the slick, and the river erupted, flames five stories high.
Luckily, it was out in under half an hour, nobody hurt.
Unluckily, a Time magazine photo of the fifty-two fire made Cleveland a national joke.

Why did it burn? Oil floats on water, and the vapor above it is what catches.
Like a greasy pan: the pan never ignites, but a flame on the oil film goes up instantly.
The outrage helped create the EPA in nineteen seventy, and the Clean Water Act in nineteen seventy-two.
Today trout swim the Cuyahoga, and kayakers paddle where flames once rose.
The river that burned is the river that came back.
